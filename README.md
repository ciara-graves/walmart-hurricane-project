# walmart-hurricane-project
DS 2002 Walmart Hurricane project. Group: Ciara Graves, Kiersten Bruns, Camden Lannin, Olympia Sauerwein
## Reflection Questions
### Q1. Data Quality Impact
Describe a specific data quality issue you encountered in the supplied data. How did your cleaning decision change the outcome of your analysis? What would have happened if you had not caught it?

**Answer:** The most common issue encountered across the data was missing data and formatting inconsistencies. For example, some of the data included an approximate sign, showing that the value was not exact. Instead of removing these values, we chose to create a separate column flagging when there was an approximate value. This allowed us to use these values in our analysis, while also being aware that there may be some inconsistencies. For missing data, we chose to leave it as Nan. We specifically did not change the values from Nan to 0, because that would skew our data and provide inaccurate analysis. We also chose to add another column to flag when the value was Nan, so that we could stay aware of the gaps within our data. These choices allowed us to use the most of our data while also being aware of potential errors and inconsistencies. 

### Q2. ETL Trade-offs
You made choices about how to standardize SKUs, handle missing timestamps, and join weather data. Pick one decision and explain: what alternative approach could you have taken, and how might it have changed your results?

**Answer:** We chose to use Open-Meteo for the weather API. We chose this API because it did not require a token/password to access it. Using a different API could have resulted in a different date range. The API we used is the backbone of our findings, so changing the API would have the largest effect on our final data analysis. With the Open-Meteo API, we used wind speed, precipitation maximums, and pressure means to find barometric data. This allowed us to identify storms hitting the Florida Walmarts. If we had used a different API then the metrics we used to identify the storms would be different. We also used an inclusive time period because an exclusive time range would be more restrictive on our data. Choosing to use an exclusive time range would have resulted in a smaller data set that could produce different findings. We chose to make the timestamps all into America, New York or Eastern Standard Time. This was to standardize the timestamps across all of our data.

### Q3. Pipeline Trust
The Walmart CDO argued that the biggest risk is not the model — it is the pipeline. Based on your experience in this project, do you agree or disagree? What was the most fragile part of your data pipeline?

**Answer:** We agree because any changes made in each step could change our final result. If we changed how we dealt with missing data then we would get a different result because our data would be smaller or larger. While category combinations played a role in organizing the data, handling missing data was the most fragile part of the pipeline. For example, 884 of the transactions had no timestamp, so by leaving them out our dataset was somewhat smaller. To mitigate risk there we did confirm that those rows were evenly spread across stores, but it still influenced what our final data looked like. For the other missing data points including coordinates, costs, and transaction information, we decided to keep them but add columns which flagged the missing data. This allowed us to preserve the data, but including them without any recognition may result in misleading data.

### Q4: Business vs. Data
For Question 5 (board games), your team had to make a recommendation with imperfect data. How did you weigh statistical evidence against business risk? Would you have decided differently with more time or more data?

**Answer:** Our final recommendation was that the Walmarts in Florida should not order more board games before storm surges, as the data we have access to does not provide enough of an impact to justify the risk. The initial findings showed an increase of $20,000 across the entire state of Florida, which is not substantial enough to make large ordering changes. When comparing units sold, a similar pattern is displayed. The jump is from 60 units to about 180 units, which shows some pattern, but not enough to justify a business risk. We think it would be worth looking into other states that are affected by natural disasters, both hurricanes and others like tornadoes. There is clearly some correlation between board games and entertainment with the storms, but a larger data set would allow us to determine more confidently what the exact correlation is. Our statistical evidence was not strong enough to justify a large business risk, as $20,000 is not largely substantial in the overall picture. Over ordering these entertainment products could result in being unable to sell them, forcing the business to reduce the price or eat the cost of the order. This is a large risk for a payout that does not seem consequential. Therefore, we decided that our statistical evidence should not be weighed heavily against a business risk.
